# A Guided Tour of the British Museum — narration transcript

## [0:00] Great Russell Street · The main entrance

Welcome to the British Museum in London, one of the greatest museums in the world. It was founded in 1753, when the doctor and collector Sir Hans Sloane left his collection of more than 71,000 objects to the nation. It opened its doors in 1759, as the first free, national, public museum in the world. The grand building in front of us was designed by Sir Robert Smirke to look like an ancient Greek temple, with 44 columns along its front. Today, the museum cares for around eight million objects, covering two million years of human history. Let's head inside.

## [0:42] Great Court · The heart of the museum

This is the Queen Elizabeth II Great Court, the heart of the museum. For nearly 150 years, this courtyard was crammed with bookshelves for the library. When the British Library moved out, architects Foster and Partners transformed it with a spectacular glass roof, which opened in the year 2000. The roof is made of 3,312 panes of glass, and no two of them are exactly the same shape. In the middle stands the round Reading Room, opened in 1857. Its readers included Karl Marx, Bram Stoker, the author of Dracula, and Mahatma Gandhi. Our first gallery is just through the doors to the west.

## [1:25] Room 4 · Egyptian sculpture

In Room 4, we find the museum's most famous object: the Rosetta Stone. It is a broken slab of dark granodiorite, carved in Egypt in 196 BC with a decree from a council of priests about the young king, Ptolemy V. What makes it special is that the same message is written three times, in three different scripts. At the top are hieroglyphs, used for important and religious texts. In the middle is Demotic, the everyday script of Egypt. And at the bottom is ancient Greek. French soldiers found the stone in 1799, near the town of Rashid, which Europeans called Rosetta. It came to the British Museum in 1802. Scholars could already read the Greek, so they used it as a key. In 1822, Jean-François Champollion announced that he had cracked the code of hieroglyphs, starting with royal names like Ptolemy, written inside oval shapes called cartouches.

## [2:24] Room 10 · Assyria: Khorsabad

Next, we step into ancient Assyria, in what is now northern Iraq. These giant guardians are called lamassu. Each has the head of a man, the body of a bull, and the wings of a bird. They once stood at the gateways of King Sargon II's palace at Khorsabad, about 2,700 years ago, to scare away evil. Now count their legs. From the front, a lamassu stands firmly still. From the side, it strides forward. To make both views work, the sculptors gave it five legs! Close by, in Room 10a, you can see the famous lion hunt carvings of King Ashurbanipal.

## [3:04] Room 18 · Greece: Parthenon

Room 18 holds sculptures from the Parthenon, the great temple of the goddess Athena, on the Acropolis in Athens. They were carved between 447 and 432 BC, under the direction of the sculptor Phidias. The long marble frieze shows a grand procession of horse riders, chariots, musicians and animals, making its way to honour Athena. Between 1801 and 1812, agents of Lord Elgin removed about half of the surviving sculptures, and in 1816 Parliament bought them for the museum. Greece has long asked for them to be returned to Athens, and the debate about where they belong continues today.

## [3:47] Room 24 · Living and dying

In Room 24 stands Hoa Hakananai'a, a statue, or moai, from Rapa Nui, also known as Easter Island, in the Pacific Ocean. He was carved from volcanic basalt around 800 to 1,000 years ago, and he stands almost two and a half metres tall. His name is often translated as 'lost or stolen friend'. Look at his back. It was later carved with birdmen, linked to a contest held each year at the ceremonial village of Orongo. In 1868, the crew of a British ship, HMS Topaze, took him from the island. Today, the Rapa Nui people have asked for him to come home.

## [4:28] Room 56 · Mesopotamia

Let's head upstairs to Room 56, and a board game that's around 4,500 years old. This is the Royal Game of Ur. It was found in a royal tomb in the ancient city of Ur, in southern Iraq, by the archaeologist Leonard Woolley in the 1920s. The board is decorated with shell, red limestone and blue lapis lazuli. Two players raced their pieces along its twenty squares. For a long time, nobody knew the rules. Then curator Irving Finkel studied a clay tablet, written much later in 177 BC, which explained how to play. Land on a rosette, and you get another turn. People still play it today!

## [5:12] Rooms 62–63 · Egyptian death and afterlife

Rooms 62 and 63 are home to some of the museum's most popular residents: the Egyptian mummies. Ancient Egyptians believed the body had to be preserved, so the spirit could live on in the afterlife. Embalmers removed the internal organs, dried the body with a salt called natron, and wrapped it in hundreds of metres of linen. The whole process could take seventy days. The body was then placed inside brightly painted coffins, covered with prayers and spells to protect it on its journey. It wasn't just people. Egyptians also mummified cats, birds and even crocodiles, as offerings to the gods.

## [5:54] Room 41 · Sutton Hoo and Europe

Now we travel to England, around 1,400 years ago. Room 41 holds the treasures of Sutton Hoo. In 1939, on Edith Pretty's land in Suffolk, Basil Brown began to uncover the outline of a 27-metre ship, buried beneath a great mound of earth. The wood had rotted away, but rows of iron rivets showed exactly where the ship had been. In the middle was a burial chamber, filled with treasure. It was probably the grave of an Anglo-Saxon king, perhaps Rædwald, ruler of East Anglia, who died around AD 624. The star object is this iron helmet. Look closely at the face: the eyebrows, nose and moustache form a dragon with outstretched wings. Edith Pretty generously gave the whole treasure to the nation.

## [6:44] Room 40 · Medieval Europe

Next door, in Room 40, meet the Lewis Chessmen. They were found in 1831 on the Isle of Lewis, in the Outer Hebrides of Scotland, and were probably carved in Norway around 850 years ago. Most are made of walrus ivory. There are kings, queens, bishops, knights, and warders, which act as the rooks. Look out for the worried-looking queens, resting their cheeks on their hands, and the fierce warriors biting their shields, known as berserkers. 82 pieces are here in London, and 11 are in the National Museum of Scotland in Edinburgh. They even inspired the wizard's chess in the first Harry Potter film!

## [7:25] Great Court · End of the tour

That's the end of our tour, but we've only seen a tiny part of the museum. Only about one per cent of the collection is on display at any one time, so there's always something new to discover. The museum also raises big questions: whose stories are being told, and where should these objects belong? Entry to the permanent collection is free. Thanks for joining the tour, and happy exploring!
